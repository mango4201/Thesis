\section{Motivation}\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Consider the task of designing a fibre-optic telecommunications network connecting major cities across a region.
The network must link all locations while minimising the total cost of laying cable.
That cost is not known when the design is fixed: it depends on terrain, since cable through mountains costs far more than cable across plains, on regulatory approvals that vary by jurisdiction, and on material prices that move with global supply chains.
Uncertainty of this kind is pervasive in infrastructure planning, and as \textcite{KouvelisYu1997} observe, forecast parameter values rarely match realised costs in practice.
A design that looks optimal under today's estimates can prove expensive under the costs that actually arrive, which raises the question this thesis takes up: how should a network be chosen when its costs become known only after the choice is made?

When the costs are known precisely, the \emph{minimum spanning tree} (MST) answers the question.
It connects all nodes at minimum total cost and is computed efficiently by well-established algorithms such as Kruskal's greedy edge selection or Prim's incremental tree growing, both running in polynomial time \cite[Chapter~6]{KorteVygen2018}.
When they are not, the MST of any one estimate carries no guarantee for the others: it may cost much more than expected once the true costs arrive, or lose out to a tree that a different estimate would have favoured.
The deterministic problem is therefore well understood and, on its own, not enough: what is wanted is a tree that is \emph{robust} to cost uncertainty.

Under uncertainty, two natural design objectives emerge.
The first is the \emph{min-max} objective, which minimises the worst-case absolute cost over all cost realisations the model admits.
It is the conservative choice, selecting a spanning tree whose cost stays controlled even in the most unfavourable circumstances.

The second is \emph{min-max regret}, which judges a tree not in absolute terms but against what could have been achieved with hindsight.
The regret of a tree in a given realisation is the difference between its cost and the cost of the best tree for that realisation.
Minimising the worst-case regret guards against a different failure: not a high absolute cost, but a choice that a better-informed planner would not have made.

This thesis models the uncertainty in three ways.
Under \emph{discrete scenario uncertainty} a planner enumerates a finite list of plausible cost vectors, each a possible future state; in the telecommunications example, these might be combinations of regulatory outcomes and material price levels.
Under \emph{interval uncertainty} each edge cost is known only to lie between a lower and an upper estimate, which suits costs that vary continuously within fixed limits, such as commodity prices or exchange rates.
Under \emph{budgeted uncertainty} every edge again has an interval, but a single parameter caps the total deviation across the graph, letting the modeller choose how conservative the analysis should be rather than commit to the full ranges at once.
That parameter interpolates: at one end the problem is the deterministic one on the nominal costs, at the other it is the interval model in full.

Two objectives and three uncertainty models give six combinations, and this thesis classifies every one of them.
The central question is this: how do computational complexity and approximability vary across the six?
\Cref{sec:intro-scope} makes the question precise and fixes the scope within which it is answered.

\section{Research Questions and Scope}\label{sec:intro-scope}

That question divides into three.

First, what is the computational complexity of the six combinations?
Does each admit a polynomial-time algorithm, or is it intractable?
Where hardness arises, does it come from the number of scenarios being unbounded, or is two scenarios already enough?
Answering this requires precise classifications: polynomial-time solvability, weak $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness, which admits pseudo-polynomial algorithms, or strong $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness, which remains hard even when every numeric value in the instance is bounded by a polynomial in the input size.

Second, where does the worst case lie inside an interval uncertainty set, and does locating it make the problem easy?
Each edge cost ranges over an interval, so the scenarios are infinitely many; if the worst of them can be named in advance, evaluating a single tree needs no search over that infinity.
Whether cheap evaluation also makes the minimisation over trees cheap is a further question, and the two objectives answer it differently.
Extremal characterisations settle the first half for both, and this thesis proves them.

Third, for problems that are computationally hard, what approximation guarantees can be achieved?
Can a polynomial-time algorithm come within a constant factor of the optimum, or within any accuracy demanded of it, as a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS) does?
Conversely, are there hardness-of-approximation results that rule out such guarantees?
A limit on what any algorithm can do is as much part of the classification as an algorithm is.

The scope of this thesis is deliberately focused.
We restrict attention to spanning tree problems in undirected, connected graphs with static, single-stage decisions: a tree is selected once, before any uncertainty is resolved, and remains fixed.
The three uncertainty models just described are defined formally in \Cref{sec:uncertainty}, and the budgeted model is analysed in \Cref{sec:mm-budgeted}.
No objectives beyond min-max and min-max regret are considered.

This thesis does not cover polyhedral uncertainty sets, ellipsoidal uncertainty, or distributional robustness, where costs follow known probability distributions.
It also excludes two-stage and recoverable models, in which decisions can be partly adjusted once realisations are observed.
\Cref{sec:outlook} returns to the last two of these, and to a generalisation of the budgeted model that replaces its single cap by several; the others lie outside this work entirely.
The aim is not encyclopaedic coverage but a self-contained treatment of the models that are in scope.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro-contributions}

This thesis delivers six principal contributions.
The first five gather established results of the field; the contribution there is the development, with fourteen results proved in full and the remainder developed from their sources or cited at the point of use.
The sixth is the thesis's own synthesis: the classification that draws them together, and the criterion that explains it.

First, we provide self-contained minimum spanning tree foundations (\Cref{ch:foundations}).
We prove five fundamental results from first principles: the fundamental cycle and cut lemmas via exchange arguments, and three MST optimality criteria (cycle property, cut property, and their equivalence).
\Cref{ch:foundations} also includes a complexity primer defining polynomial time, $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness (weak and strong), and approximation concepts (constant-factor algorithms, FPTAS, PTAS) for classifying the robust variants.

Second, we establish extremal characterisations for interval uncertainty (\Cref{ch:minmax,ch:regret}).
For min-max, \Cref{lem:interval-extremal-cost} shows that the vector of upper bounds is a worst case for every tree at once, reducing the problem to one deterministic MST computation.
For regret, \Cref{lem:interval-extremal-regret} names a worst case for each individual tree, charging every edge of that tree its upper bound and every other edge its lower bound.
That worst case therefore moves with the tree, and the minimisation over trees stays hard.
Both lemmas carry complete proofs, as do the two corollaries that draw their algorithmic consequences.

Third, we prove that min-max stays polynomial under budgeted uncertainty (\Cref{sec:mm-budgeted}).
The worst case again depends on the tree here, since the budget is spent on the edges of the chosen tree, and \Cref{thm:mm-budgeted-poly} removes that dependence cheaply: fixing a single deviation level restores an edge-by-edge cost for every tree, and at most $|E| + 1$ levels need to be tried.
The result is due to \textcite{BertsimasSim2003}; the two supporting lemmas and the theorem are proved here in full.

Fourth, we give two representative hardness proofs (\Cref{ch:minmax,ch:regret}).
\Cref{thm:mm-k2-hard} establishes weak $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness of min-max spanning trees with two discrete scenarios by reduction from the partition problem, on a grid graph whose scenario costs encode target subset sums; the proof carries the construction, the correctness argument and the encoding analysis.
\Cref{thm:regret-k2-hard} reuses that construction unaltered and adjusts only the analysis.
Under discrete scenarios the two objectives carry the same complexity at every scenario count, though once $K$ is part of the input they no longer share an approximation guarantee.

Fifth, we present the 2-approximation for interval regret spanning trees (\Cref{ch:regret}).
\Cref{thm:regret-2approx} states that a minimum spanning tree under the midpoint costs has maximum regret at most twice that of an optimal tree; the algorithm is due to \textcite{KasperskiZielinski2006}, and its argument is developed in the text rather than proved.
No better guarantee is known for any combinatorial problem of this kind.
Whether any algorithm can improve on the factor $2$, for a min-max regret problem with interval costs whose deterministic version is solvable in polynomial time, remains an open question in the literature \cite[Open Problem~2]{Goerigk2021RCO}.

Sixth, we draw the results together (\Cref{ch:conclusion}).
\Cref{tab:complexity-landscape} records each combination with its complexity class and the guarantees available for it, and the one combination \Cref{ch:regret} leaves aside, min-max regret under a budget, is settled there, its strong hardness inherited from the interval case.
Across the table min-max and regret separate on complexity under intervals and under a budget alike.
\Cref{sec:synthesis} accounts for that with a single criterion: what decides complexity is the price of making the adversary's worst case independent of the chosen tree.
That price is nothing under intervals and one enumerated level under a budget, and under discrete scenarios it cannot be paid at all unless $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{NP}$.

A fixed four-vertex micro-graph runs through the thesis, carrying interval costs, the discrete scenarios read off them, and budgeted parameters drawn from the same data.
\Cref{sec:gallery} solves it under every combination the thesis develops.
The two objectives disagree on this instance: min-max returns one tree and regret another in every panel, and reading every interval at its midpoint and solving the deterministic problem returns the regret answer, not the min-max one.
\Cref{fig:micro-graph-tradeoff} places all eight spanning trees by worst-case cost and maximum regret, and shows that only those two are ever selected.

\section{Thesis Structure}\label{sec:intro-structure}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.

\Cref{ch:foundations} establishes mathematical foundations, proving minimum spanning tree optimality criteria from first principles and defining the robust optimisation framework (uncertainty models, objectives, complexity terminology).

\Cref{ch:minmax} analyses the min-max objective across all three uncertainty models, characterising worst-case costs under intervals, establishing computational complexity for discrete scenarios across different values of the scenario count parameter, and proving polynomial solvability under a budget.

\Cref{ch:regret} examines the min-max regret objective, which measures relative performance rather than absolute cost.
Interval regret is shown to be harder than interval min-max despite sharing extremal properties, while discrete regret mirrors the complexity hierarchy of discrete min-max.

\Cref{ch:conclusion} sets the two objectives side by side: it consolidates the results of \Cref{ch:minmax,ch:regret} into a classification table and solves the running micro-graph in an example gallery.
It then answers the question posed in \Cref{sec:intro-motivation} and turns, in \Cref{sec:outlook}, to what lies beyond the scope fixed above and to two open questions the literature leaves standing.

\Cref{app:notation} collects the notation of the thesis in a single table, grouped by category, with the section introducing each symbol.
